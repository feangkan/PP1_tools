# Week 07

## Topic

**The Architect as Worker** (Helen Duong) + **Union for Architectural Workers** (Tom Marshall, President of the UAW, Senior Associate at Fieldwork). Two paired lectures that introduce the worker identity architects rarely construct their professional identity around: they receive a salary, work for an organisation, and their employer sells architectural services that depend on their time and expertise. Helen sets up the structural argument (individual "good employer/good employee" behaviour can't fix a system-wide race to the bottom); Tom follows with the union's account of the economics of architectural labour and the case for collective, rather than individual, action.

## Learning goals

- Hold "professional" and "worker" as two identities operating at once, and ask when professional commitment becomes labour.
- Distinguish individual responses to poor conditions (negotiate, change practice, become the employer, leave the profession) from structural ones (organising, collective bargaining, regulatory/institutional change) — and why "if we changed the people but kept the same incentives, would the outcome change?" is the diagnostic question.
- Understand the five areas the Australian Wellbeing of Architects research links to working conditions (value and worth, time and money, trust and transparency, risk and responsibility, psychological/psychosocial safety) — only one of which is about individual mental health.
- Trace Future Architecture Front's (FAF) path from anecdote → shared awareness ("class consciousness building") → statistics → open letter → workplace organising → institutional governance (the 2022 election of Muyiwa Oki as RIBA president), and why FAF still worked *with* the Section of Architectural Workers rather than treating one visible leadership win as sufficient.
- Learn the union's framing of the economics: developer profit margins, the ~2% architectural fee on subsidised-housing projects, the ~25% billable-rate return to the person doing the work, and how these figures connect fee competition to working conditions.
- Apply "professional literacy" — matching a problem to the mechanism capable of changing it (employer, practice, professional body, regulator, client/procurement, movement, union) — to the manifesto (who decides, who votes, who controls resources, who can change the instrument, who looks powerful but is actually constrained).

## Key terms

| Term | Definition (in your words) |
|------|----------------------------|
| The worker identity | The architect as salaried employee whose labour is sold by their employer — an identity the profession rarely builds its self-image around, next to student → graduate → registered architect → director |
| Structural vs individual explanation | A poor outcome (unpaid overtime, fee cutting) explained by incentives and system design, not just individual bad actors — "if we changed the people but kept the same incentives, would the outcome change?" |
| Collective labour | Architecture is produced by architects, graduates, technicians, BIM staff, admin, finance, communications — not the mythologised solo "autonomous architect" |
| Wellbeing of Architects — five areas | Value and worth, time and money, trust and transparency, risk and responsibility, psychological/psychosocial safety — only the last is about individual mental health; the rest are about how work is organised |
| Race to the bottom | Competing practices underbidding each other on fee to win work, eventually reducing the resourcing available to do the work properly — a structural market feature, not an accumulation of individual moral failures (Charlie Edmonds) |
| Class consciousness building (FAF) | Turning private, individually-felt frustration into a recognised *shared* condition — anecdote → statistics → open letter → connecting people to organisations already capable of acting, rather than FAF itself becoming "the" vehicle for change |
| Professional agency as an ecosystem | No single mechanism (employer, practice, professional body, regulator, client, movement, union) fixes everything; effective action needs connections between them, matched to the specific problem |
| Professional literacy | Being able to identify what problem is being raised, at what scale, who has authority over it, who benefits from the status quo, and which mechanism could actually change it |
