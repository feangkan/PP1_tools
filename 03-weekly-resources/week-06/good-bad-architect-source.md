## B. The Good and the Bad Architect (lines 37–66)

- Two woodcuts, *Le Premier Tome de l'Architecture*, 1567 (Philibert de l'Orme). Good architect: four hands, three eyes, four ears — "not just an accumulation of knowledge, but a capacity to see, to listen, to interpret, and to act and transmit that knowledge." Small wings on the feet = rests on nature "in a light and considerate way." Bad architect: no eyes/nose/hands, chaotic setting, wide-open mouth. (lines 39–49)
- "The good architect … is not merely compliant. They understand the system in which they practice and they exercise judgment about when established practice is inadequate." (line 51)
- **"who is the client not in the room?"** — occupants, future users, the country, the environment, the broader public. Professional trust depends on architects weighing these even when they're not in the commercial transaction. (lines 53–55)
- "Registration might establish a minimum threshold … but it does not define the profession's ambitions." Professional judgment is what you exercise when a prescribed procedure doesn't resolve the situation. (lines 57–59)
- The *collective*: "to be consistent with other architects, maintaining the standards and integrity of the profession … extracted from the architects' regulations." (line 63)

## C. Kuhn — normal science / crisis / paradigm shift (lines 67–86)

- Development happens via phases: normal science ("business as usual") → discrepancies resolved incrementally → accumulation of anomalies → crisis → revolutionary change → new paradigm. (lines 67–69)
- Two uses: (1) architecture's own acts in the cycle become clearer; (2) example — condensation/moisture failures appear as anomalies in accepted practice; repeated failures prompt research/inquiry/debate; evidence alters guidance in the **National Construction Code**; "What was once an exception becomes part of normal professional practice." (lines 71–75)
- **"an architect does not only respond to rules, the profession can help produce the knowledge through which rules and ordinary practice might change."** (line 75)
- "anomalies, inconsistencies, irregularities and contradictions are not just problems but clues for potential change." (line 85)

## D. Innovation = shortening the production process; where the benefit goes (lines 79–122)

- Mobile phone example: fewer components, lighter, more powerful; features integrated (shell + charging + wireless + Bluetooth); each innovation began as a research project; each closes the "research gap" and shortens the production line. (lines 81–95)
- **"An innovation can be defined as any shortening of this production process. The gap could be defined as the research gap and could be expressed in terms of benefit."** (line 93)
- Apartment buildings now built with fewer components, manufactured off-site, delivered as parts. (line 83)
- But: "phone prices have stopped decreasing, meaning the benefits of innovation are now … increasingly going to shareholders rather than being passed on to consumers." (line 101)
- **"where does the benefit go?"** Three scenarios (pie charts): (1) balanced cost/price — one ideal business model; (2) the profession shares the innovation — **Newcastle University open-sourced COVID-19 treatment research, collaborated with global labs → tested/developed by pharma → taught to doctors → standard care**: "The profession collectively owns that knowledge, passing it on through education … ensuring innovation is accessible to both the profession and eventually to the public." (lines 105–111); (3) the **entrepreneur model** — like Apple: innovations protected as IP, iPhone production cost reduced but savings not passed on. (lines 113–115)
- "The professional question is who ultimately captures the value of innovation? … The problem arises when that knowledge is treated only as proprietary difference and the wider profession is prevented from learning from it." (lines 119–121)
- "growing evidence that the architectural profession is adopting an entrepreneurial approach, often supporting monopolistic conditions … contradicts the profession's code of conduct, architecture regulations, and its claim of ethical conduct, altruism and public responsibility." (line 125)
- Examples: competing on "the long speech" / on building time; standardised multi-res housing done "cheaply" on spreadsheets + BIM + a facade; a dominant heritage-architecture practice that becomes "rule makers rather than questioning that." (lines 127–137)

## E. The pathway from an individual anomaly to normal practice (lines 143–153)

- practitioner observes an anomaly → sustained inquiry + research/evidence → **monitored** → **shared outside the practice** (even just "this isn't working for us right now — not enough fees, not enough time", without disclosing project confidences) → professional debate → publication + dissemination → institutional adoption → education + guidance → standards + codes → normal practice again. (lines 147–151)
- **"This is the machinery through which a profession learns. Sharing knowledge is therefore not simply altruism, it is part of how architecture develops as a discipline and continues to remain relevant."** (line 153)

## F. Privatisation of professional knowledge (lines 155–186)

- Above-the-line = open/free: research papers, the **National Construction Code**, Australian Standards. Below-the-line = paid licensed products: certification, validation, testing. (lines 155–159)
- **Standards Australia → SAI Global:** advisory notes once made public by Melbourne Uni + CSIRO; Standards Australia progressively sold from 40% government ownership down to zero; "where information was once accessible, it is now private intellectual property, i.e., you pay for access." Direct effect: performance tests for flammable cladding privately owned → impediment to certifying imported products. (lines 161–165)
- **Revit / BIM licences:** disciplinary knowledge (drafting + product knowledge + building technology) once taught as separate university subjects is now embedded in proprietary software firms pay to licence; "many architectural firms act as … front-end agents for these systems." (lines 169–173)
- Same with green-building certification (mis-transcribed "RIM/LEAD" = **BREEAM / LEED / Green Star / NABERS** type schemes) — registration + assessment fees pay for research already done. (line 175)
- **"when a method becomes a black box, professional agency is reduced. Architects may become users of a process without being able to interrogate the assumptions that it produces."** (line 185)

## G. Badiou / commerce and science (lines 187–216)

- Alain Badiou (transcribed "Alan Badou/Verdu"): art "in the servants of commerce does not give true value … a form of distortion." "It's not technology that one should rail against, but science being subjected to the system of its possible practical outcomes … its subjection to the capitalist mode of production." (lines 187–197)
- "you can run the practice, the profession can remain, but at the same time, is it purely about what can be used to make money? … How can those two things be also part of the greater good … the research and the sharing of knowledge. And that's what distinguishes a profession from an occupation." (lines 205–209)
- If value were "owned by the community of the profession and its value passed on to the public," progress would be "incremental and over time generate something that everybody could benefit from." (lines 213–215)

## H. Designing the profession, not just the buildings — Easterling (lines 217–253)

- Keller Easterling (transcribed "Keller S. Stone"): growth suburb / Port of Melbourne / Shenzhen SEZ — the built outcome is "the product of a big system, as much as the product of individual designers." (lines 219–225)

- Anomaly → normal practice must "travel through … institutions and instruments, through research, professional debate, education standards, codes and contracts. These are not just constraints … they are mechanisms through which architecture is produced and changed." (lines 243–245)
