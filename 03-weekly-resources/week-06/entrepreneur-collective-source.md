## Part 1 — `Week 6 PP1 lecture AI .txt`

### Framing (lines 1–21)
- "technology itself isn't fundamentally bad … there's two issues. One is that technology tends to have very unintended consequences. And … technology in this age of extreme capitalism is problematic. … capitalism … concentrates wealth and power. And the combination of AI and capitalism is gonna be very, very complicated." (lines 3–7)
- **Competition removes the choice:** "when we talk about … whether the efficiency gains … flow to you … or the practice or … the community — I don't think there's going to be many cases where you're … making that decision. The market is going to make that decision for you because you have to compete." Large practices adopt the tools, deliver at lower fees, and "you will decide whether … your business [can] cooperate in that market." (lines 9–17)
- Offers "a vaguely optimistic look" and wants students to "differentiate between ways to use AI that will benefit you and ways that won't." (lines 19–21)

### History (lines 45–92)
- Andrej Karpathy's 2015 blog — machines generating text that looks human (fake Shakespeare, fake code); at the time consensus was these were "innately human." (lines 49–57)
- 2015 NGV pavilion proposal: a ticket booth prints a design idea; visitors build it from inflatable blocks "designed to be stolen" and redistributed as pool furniture. (lines 65–69)
- Bot trained on every ArchDaily article → generated text reveals "just how thin so much architectural writing is." Now "not only the ArchDaily article but also the whole design and all of the videos … can be generated." (lines 73–83)
- "most people wouldn't be able to tell the difference" between an AI-generated project and an award-winning one. "our discipline is kind of quite conceited in that way." (line 83)

### The scary stuff (lines 95–118)
- Models are "surprisingly human" — yelling at ChatGPT lights up parts of the network that model fear/self-doubt/sadness; "when you threaten Claude … Claude gets really, really worried." (lines 95–104)
- **Misalignment / paperclip maximiser:** a model asked to write a great manifesto might "hack into … the architect registration board" to do it; "the really fancy great new versions … aren't getting released because the models have a tendency to go do that kind of thing." (lines 109–118)

### What's actually valuable now (lines 119–199)
- Most people only use AI as a chatbot ("really, really good auto-complete" + trained to be conversational, 2022). "none of you have actually used it in the way that's exciting." (lines 123–131)
- The shift: AI writing software (napkin sketch → working website, 2024) → **AI operating your computer** (2025, "the reason that Anthropic is a massive company"). "a lot of people … say, if AI will be able to do everything that humans can do on computers … that's obviously worth a lot of money." (lines 133–139)
- Informal class poll: ~everyone uses ChatGPT; ~10% have used AI to control their computer; ~8 people have made software with it; **1 person** has tried to sell something. "You should all be doing this bottom thing. Every single person." (lines 141–157)
- **"the actual value of AI for you right now is to automate things you do on your computer … that is absolutely transformational and nobody's doing it."** (line 157)
- Value going **up**: wisdom, experience, initiative, agency. Value going **down**: technical skills ("being good at Revit … worthless"), knowledge work ("most of knowledge work is just doing stuff on your computer and that's gonna get automated"). (lines 181–189)
- **The formula:** "know what needs doing" **+** "AI can do it for you" **=** "you can benefit." The catch: "to know what needs doing you need the experience." (lines 193–199)
- **Pulled-up career ladder:** "it's going to be harder and harder to get the experience that you need in order to then go benefit from AI." (line 199)

### What not to do / what to do (lines 203–263)
- **Reverse centaur:** centaur = you're the brains driving the machine, AI is horsepower. Reverse centaur = "the AI is doing all of the thinking and you are just … the meat sack moving stuff around." "You do not want to outsource any thinking or any learning." (lines 203–213)
- "Never ever ever ask ChatGPT for any ideas". (line 221)
- **"You should use AI for leverage. That's about it. … apply AI to a problem that you have … Find the problem that lots and lots of people have and apply AI to that. Using AI on your own assignment doesn't really help anyone."** (lines 223–225)
- How to benefit in an AI world: aspire to / be obsessed by something (needs goals to give the AI); learn from experts / acquire disciplinary knowledge (expect **unpaid internships** to replace paid graduate learning); get good at noticing problems; "it's hard to go find a job but it's easy to start a business"; get good at things AI can't verify ("how does this make you feel"), interpersonal skills, care, art; "skate where the puck is moving"; **"just make things."** (lines 217–263)

## Part 2 — `Week 6 PP1 lecture AI part 2.txt` (Q&A, Gwyl + Helen)

### Graduate jobs (lines 1–8, 199–213, 225–237)
- Gwyl: "very worried. I expect fewer opportunities for graduates." Much graduate work in large practices is "repetitive tasks … already able to be automated"; a practice "isn't gonna be altruistic … to hire a human … a hundred times slower … and cost a hundred times more … they won't because of competition." (lines 3–7)
- But near-term: "when you guys graduate, there will be a ton of jobs … the year after next, I reckon there'll still be some jobs." The point is to be the graduate who already knows how to delegate computer tasks to agents. (lines 199–213, 235)
- Student (angry): "Does this profession want a future? … four out of five of us are never going to register. … Why are we being told to adopt the tool that is going to replace us …?" "You can't use a tool made unethically in an ethical manner." (lines 225–231)
- Helen: this subject is deliberately not "go be an architect, architecture is great" — it's "a vehicle to try and unpack and understand the profession," and the assignments are where you "critique what … you don't believe is going to be beneficial." (lines 247–253)

### "Wait them out?" — the AI bubble (lines 9–24, 37–52)
- Tom's question: LLM companies are hugely unprofitable (Ed Zitron / Cory Doctorow); could the profession just wait for them to go bankrupt? (lines 9–13)
- Gwyl: "the companies will go bust, but … the cat's out of the bag." Analogy: the telecom firms that laid internet fibre "massively overinvested … went broke before they could get that money back. But we still use the internet." The model capability already exists. (lines 13–24)
- On the report that ~87% of firms won't renew AI contracts: depends whether they used it for something it's good at. In software engineering / maths ("canaries in the coal mine" for verifiable work), the last 12 months went from "check every little bit of code" to "not checking any … because it is suddenly reliable" — "utterly transformative" in that domain. (lines 37–52)
- **Design resists this:** "good design operates without clear definitions of what is correct or incorrect. You're redefining the problem while solving the problem … that's a classic thing that AI is currently really, really bad at." So: "use AI for what it's really good at … but you should keep being the designer." (lines 53–60)

### Privatised, architecture-specific AI tools (lines 61–84)
- Concern: AI embedded in proprietary product-specification / BIM tools = the same privatisation problem Helen raised, now "tied up in an unaccessible tool unless you pay for it." (lines 65–70)
- Helen: the collective question — "what does paying institute membership … get you that a small practice would not"? If the profession doesn't provide shared access, "you would have … some companies that have access and other companies that don't," widening disparity. (lines 71–75)
- Gwyl: "Revit's days are numbered … every single practice above a certain size will just have their own fully custom way of modeling buildings" because making software is now nearly free. "large practices will have this first … Small practices won't." A power dynamic. (lines 77–84)

### Why knowledge work loses value (lines 85–96)
- "the difference between knowledge and wisdom … a lot of knowledge work … there's a process that you just follow, there's a right or wrong outcome … That'll just be automated away." Wisdom — "knowing whether … that's the right process in the first place, knowing when to challenge that process" — comes from experience/judgment/expertise and stays valuable. "a director of a practice is going to benefit from AI way more than … anyone else in the practice." (lines 89–95)

### Polls (lines 97–147)
- "as a future graduate, which kind of work would be most important for developing your expertise?" — technical work **2%**; problem solving / learning to make judgments **~20%**; collaborative work / learning from experienced people **0%**; "don't know / it'll all be even" **78%**. Helen: the 0% for collaborative work "is really problematic" — "How are you going to learn things that require judgment, expertise and knowledge from someone else?" (lines 97–113)
- "what possibility created by AI are you most excited about?" — "more time" (most); "greater capability, doing things I couldn't previously do" ~20%; "new ways to practice, new roles, services and businesses" ~15%; **"greater impact / tackling larger social, environmental, industry problems" ~2%.** (lines 143–147)

### Ethics of using AI at all (lines 135–169)
- Helen: separate the **citizen** ("concerned about the environment, exploitation, data centres … that's personal") from the **architect professional hat** ("you're entering an industry, you want to do something with your degree — a different set of questions"). "there are no real right or wrong answers to this, but this is why we're having this conversation." (lines 139–153)
- Gwyl: "the most important thing with AI is to just not ignore it. The risk of ignoring it is really high … the cost of engaging … is really low. … if we all choose to ignore it, then our voice doesn't … contribute to the discourse." "ethically there are tons of things to be really concerned about … The biggest one being … it is the ultimate form of capitalist extraction … extracted everybody's IP and concentrated [it in] a thing like Anthropic and OpenAI." But refusing to engage "doesn't actually help all of those people who've been disenfranchised by it either." (lines 155–163)
- "AI is not just a tool … it's gonna so rapidly change how we work so quickly." (lines 167–169)

### Who checks the AI's work? (lines 171–197, 303–309)
- Gwyl on access risk: "it's no different to an employee … you don't give it the company credit card … you don't give it an account that can delete all your files." (lines 215–221)
- Helen (ARBV/RMIT survey she's running): current policy "always … puts the responsibility for AI use and the checking of it on the individual." She argues that's wrong for a graduate — "the idea that a graduate is responsible for checking their own work because they're being told to do things faster makes absolutely no sense." Knowledge-checking "needs to be spread out … People who are experienced need to be used if AI is used." A business should say "graduate, use it, be fast. We need to check it, I'll show you how to check it." Like an engineering documentation set, "somebody else looks at it … a fresh set of eyes." (lines 177–195)
- The ARBV/RMIT research: how many use AI, what for, who asks them to, is there a policy, who's responsible for checking, impact on compliance — "Those questions haven't even been asked yet." Results expected ~March–April next year. Contributors: RMIT + ARBV + professional bodies + practices + universities "everybody agreed that they should all be doing it." (lines 30–34, 257–261, 303–309)

### On education / architectural training (lines 291–301)
- "architectural education is really useful … in a world where there's more AI automation … being a generalist is quite useful … the design studio … where you are defending ideas … these are skills that are really hard to get from other forms of education." "question whether the problem you're solving is actually the most important problem." "all of the stuff that I build is just solving my own problems, and then you hope other people have the same problems — which is what all of you should be doing." (lines 297–301)
