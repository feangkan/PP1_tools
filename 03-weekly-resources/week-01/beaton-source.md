## Beaton’s core definition of professionalism

Professionalism = combination of:

1. **Knowledge** and **skills** (head + hand)
2. **Trustworthiness** and **altruism** (heart)
3. Commitment to a **life of service** to others
4. Summation / hallmark: **integrity**

Key mechanism: **asymmetrical knowledge** (professional knows more than the client) → **power** → duty to use that power **ethically** for client **and** public good.

## “A profession, they say” — 11 characteristics (via Beaton)

A profession:

1. confers status within society  
2. organises itself into some sort of professional body  
3. is learned — prolonged and specialised training and education  
4. is altruistic (service rather than profit)  
5. offers autonomy within the job role  
6. is informed by an ethical code of some kind  
7. is non-commercial  
8. has collective influence within society  
9. is self-regulatory  
10. is collegial  
11. is client-focused  

### Profession vs business / trade (Beaton’s contrast)

| Profession (ideal) | Business / trade / guild (contrast) |
|--------------------|-------------------------------------|
| Altruistic + ethical duty | Primarily commercial / profit |
| Protects clients + society | Protects members’ market interests |
| Code of ethics beyond personal morals | Quality/price control without same public duty |
| Client- and public-focused | Member- and market-focused |

Beaton also notes profit and service are not always incompatible — professionalism fails when **profit trumps altruism**.

## Classic professions vs neo-professions

| Idea | Content |
|------|---------|
| Classic “learned” professions | Historically divinity, law, medicine (sometimes + architecture, military, teaching) |
| Proliferation | Specialisation expands → many more occupations claim professional status |
| Neo-professions | Newer specialised fields (e.g. IT services, business management) seeking professional recognition |
| Architecture in Beaton | Named among **design professions** alongside engineering |

Useful class angle: Is architecture closer to law/medicine (strong regulation, ethical monopoly, public duty) or to a neo-profession under commercial pressure?
