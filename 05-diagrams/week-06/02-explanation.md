## One-sentence overview

> This diagram shows innovation as a neutral production cycle that becomes a *professional* question the moment you ask where the benefit goes — and it maps the machinery the profession uses to turn private discovery into shared knowledge, the ways that machinery is being privatised, and AI as the sharpest live instance of the whole problem.

## How to read the diagram (order)

1. Start at the **hub question** — innovation shortens production, but who captures the benefit, and does the profession learn from it?
2. Follow the **production cycle** loop — this is Helen's actual definition of innovation: *any shortening of the production process*. Each loop closes a "research gap"; the next loop is shorter.
3. Move right to **where the benefit goes** — the same innovation splits three ways: balanced price, the collective (public benefit), the entrepreneur (proprietary IP). Only the third is framed as contradicting the profession's code.
4. Drop to the **collective machinery** spine — the eight-step pathway from one practitioner's anomaly to a change in standards/codes. This is what "the collective" concretely *is*.
5. Read the **privatisation stack** as the counter-force — each item disables a stage of the machinery.
6. Read the **systems** cluster (Easterling) as the reframe — the rules and standards are themselves design objects ("active forms").
7. End at **AI**, drawn last because it re-poses every cluster's question in the present tense.

## Cluster-by-cluster

### Production cycle — what "innovation" means here
- **What it means:** design/build → notice an anomaly → research closes the gap → fewer components / less time / less labour → next cycle shorter → repeat. Kuhn sits here: anomalies are "clues for potential change," not just problems.
- **Why it's on the diagram:** it's the load-bearing definition. Without it, "innovation" stays a slogan ("we should do more research" — which Helen explicitly rejects in the Assessment 2 briefing).
- **Link to other clusters:** the closed research gap is a *benefit* — which hands straight to the next cluster.
- **Worked example (moved off the diagram):** condensation/moisture failures show up as anomalies in otherwise-accepted building practice; repeated failures prompt research and debate; evidence changes NCC guidance; the exception becomes normal practice.

### Where the benefit goes — three models
- **What it means:** Model 1, balanced cost/price. Model 2, *the collective* — open-sourced, taught, becomes standard practice, reaches the public (Helen's example: a university open-sourcing medical research that becomes standard care — institution/treatment to verify). Model 3, *the entrepreneur* — innovation protected as IP, production cost falls, savings held (Apple/iPhone).
- **Why it's on the diagram:** this single split is the lecture's spine and Assessment 2's spine. It's also the honest place to say the manifesto "can support entrepreneurship while still asking how innovation contributes to the profession and the public."
- **Link:** Model 2 *depends upon* the machinery spine being travelled. Model 3 *causes* monopolistic drift — competing on "the long speech" and build-time, standardised housing done cheaply, sector rule-makers.

### The collective machinery — how a profession learns
- **What it means:** anomaly → inquiry + evidence → **monitored & shared outside the practice** → professional debate → publication → institutional adoption → education → standards + codes → normal practice (→ loops).
- **Why it's on the diagram:** it's the most reusable thing in the week. Every "maintain/refine" manifesto point is a defence of one of these stages; every "change" point is a repair of one.
- **Link:** privatising *any* stage breaks the chain — which is the next cluster. "Sharing knowledge is … not simply altruism, it is part of how architecture develops as a discipline."

### Privatisation of knowledge
- **What it means:** open (research papers, the NCC, Australian Standards) vs paid (certification, testing, licences). Standards Australia → SAI Global (government ownership 40% → 0%; public advisory notes become paywalled IP; flammable-cladding test data now private). Revit/BIM licences embed disciplinary knowledge firms must rent. Paid green-certification schemes.
- **Why it's on the diagram:** it's the concrete, Australian, checkable evidence that the machinery is being disabled — not a hypothetical.
- **Link:** ends at the **black box** — "when a method becomes a black box, professional agency is reduced. Architects may become users of a process without being able to interrogate the assumptions."

### Design the profession, not just buildings (Easterling)
- **What it means:** the growth suburb, the Port of Melbourne, the Shenzhen SEZ are shaped by planning controls, standards, finance, contracts, incentives as much as by designers. "The action is the form." Shared standards *are* infrastructure. New descriptions of architecture ask what it *does* (the Norwegian tourist-route car parks specified by performance guidance and a surveyor's sticks, to protect trees).
- **Why it's on the diagram:** it gives the manifesto permission to propose changing *rules and instruments*, not just buildings — and names that as core professional work, not activism on the side.
- **Link:** a black-boxed tool is an "active form" working on the architect; the anomaly-to-practice journey must "travel through institutions and instruments."

### AI — makes every question concrete
- **What it means:** AI shortens production dramatically (= innovation). But: 10 hrs → 1 hr — whose benefit? Confidence (generating an answer) ≠ professional judgment (knowing it's appropriate/correct/in the public interest). Jahn: use AI as *leverage* on real, widely-held problems, not on your own coursework; don't become a *reverse centaur* (AI thinks, you execute); never outsource thinking or learning; value rises for wisdom/experience/agency and falls for technical skill and rote knowledge work; the experience ladder is being pulled up (repetitive graduate work automates first → unpaid internships). Helen: **who checks the output** must be collective, not dumped on the individual graduate told to go faster.
- **Why it's drawn last:** it isn't a new topic — it's the present-tense version of the whole diagram. Competition (Jahn's point) means individuals mostly can't *choose* where the value flows; the market chooses. That is exactly why it's a *profession-level* question — a manifesto point, not a personal preference.
- **Citizen vs architect hat (Helen):** concerns about extraction, data centres and the environment are real and personal; the professional question is narrower — what will you use it for, and who is accountable for checking it.

## Critical point (your position — Direction)

- **Tension shown:** the profession has a real, working machinery for converting private discovery into shared knowledge (research → debate → publication → adoption → education → codes), and that machinery is being quietly disabled from three directions — privatised standards, rented black-box tools, and now AI whose value capture and accountability default to the individual and the platform.
- **What should change:** a manifesto should name which stages of the collective machinery are worth defending as they are (open standards, the NCC as amendable, peer review, professional debate, CPD as formative learning) and which need rebuilding for the AI/entrepreneur era (collective checking of AI-assisted work; shared or institute-held access to architecture-specific AI tools so disparity between large and small practices doesn't widen; a protected route to disciplinary experience that doesn't depend on unpaid internships; explicit rules on who captures the benefit of practice-level innovation).

## If someone asks "so what?"

Innovation isn't the issue — *value capture and accountability* are. The profession already knows how to turn a private discovery into shared professional knowledge; the honest question for practice today is which parts of that pathway we defend and which we rebuild, now that the tools doing the innovating are owned by someone else and the benefit defaults to whoever owns them.
