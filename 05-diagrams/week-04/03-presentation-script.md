# 03 — Presentation script: Week 04 — The Good and the Bad Architect

- **Paired diagram:** `01-diagram.md`
- **Length:** ~3–4 minutes spoken
- **Use:** tutorial talk-through, not verbatim reading

---

**Opening (Core):**

"This week starts with a 1910 essay by Adolf Loos: a villa 'desecrates' a peaceful lake regardless of whether its architect is good or bad — 'before God there are no good or bad architects, in His presence all architects are equal.' I've used that as the test for everything else this week. Because in Victoria, the Architects Act only asks for one minimum standard — good character, two years' practice — for every registered architect. So what is the profession's 'good architect vs bad architect' machinery actually measuring, if not that?"

**Awards (Awards + Counter-models):**

"The first place that machinery shows up is awards. The AIA runs sixteen-plus categories, and the lecture asks directly: are these different *architectures*, or different *business models*? The evidence that categories change outcomes is historical — the 1969 Southland Shopping Centre beat the now-iconic NGV for the top Victorian award, back when there were no categories at all, judged purely on merit. I've also put three real counter-models next to it: the Social Impact Award's non-architect-majority jury, the Learning Environments Australia awards — which almost never pick the same school building the AIA does — and the Indigenous-awards debate, where the real question isn't 'should there be a separate award' but 'should Indigenous knowledge sit inside every criterion instead.'"

**Voices:**

"Then I lined up four critics across almost forty years, because none of this is new. Don Bailey in 1985 called awards 'corporate narcissism.' Rory Hyde in 2011 found they 'celebrate success, punish ambition.' Nigel Shaw, AIA National President in 1999, said the public sees architects, not architecture. And Naomi Stead, writing in 2024 on the magazine's 120th year, called it a 'walled garden' that passed over things like the Apology to the Stolen Generations and Black Saturday almost entirely — a searchlight, not a lantern."

**Media ecosystem + Power apparatus:**

"I mapped six media channels — awards, journals, online design media, practice PR, Instagram/TikTok, LinkedIn — each with a different gatekeeper and a different risk. The Bates Smart Media Award makes this concrete across just three years: 2023 rewarded a collective Indigenous knowledge charter, 2025 rewarded an individual-career monograph, 2026 rewarded a TikTok advocacy account. That's the whole tension of the week, playing out inside one prize. Underneath all of it sits the same small, overlapping network — the lecture reuses the 'TheyRule' corporate-board-interlock visual to make the point that the same people sit across schools, the Institute, and the registration board at once."

**Closing (Collective test):**

"The lecture ends with its own test: does an award, a magazine feature, a campaign make architecture more useful, more equitable, more publicly accountable — or does it just make one architect more marketable? And its own list of what the profession risks ignoring while it celebrates itself names housing, climate, country, labour conditions, *procurement*, and public trust. That's actually the first time procurement shows up anywhere in this toolkit with a real source behind it — which matters, because it's one of Assessment 1's required topics."
