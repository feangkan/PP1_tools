## One-sentence overview

> This diagram maps the Disciplinary Matrix around one heavily gated organising spine — the Registration Pathway, Victoria's actual path to the title "Architect" — surrounded by the six required-topic clusters, a newly built Procurement cluster still attached by a half-finished connector, and a deliberately small, backgrounded Power Structure cluster — because the profession polices entry to the one pathway everyone can see far more tightly than it polices who actually gets to write the rules (legislation) or keep design control after a project is handed to a contractor (procurement).

## Why plain, brief-matching cluster names

An earlier draft used a theme-park metaphor (rides, lands, a control booth) to make the hierarchy-of-visibility argument land visually. That's been dropped: the diagram now names every cluster directly after the brief's own required topics — Registration Pathway, Education, Employment, Legislation, Professional Bodies, Ethics & Public Value, Research & Innovation, Procurement — so the diagram answers "relate education, employment, registration, legislation, professional bodies, ethics/codes, procurement, research, public value, innovation" in the brief's own words, not through a narrative skin a marker has to decode first. The underlying argument and hierarchy (one dominant, tightly-gated spine; required topics around it; a critical-position cluster drawn deliberately less prominent) is unchanged — only the labelling and framing language changed. See `01-diagram.md`'s v07 redraw note.

## How to read the diagram (order)

1. **The hub question** — what does it take to be trusted with the title Architect, and trusted by whom?
2. **The Registration Pathway** — the required organising spine: education → logged experience → APE → ARBV registration → CPD/PI insurance, with a contingency loop for failure and an exclusion path for serious breach.
3. **The five required-topic clusters** — Education, Employment, Legislation, Professional Bodies, Ethics & Public Value — each a required Assessment 1 topic, each connected to the pathway and to each other by cross-links.
4. **Research & Innovation** — carrying Week 5's pre-reading material: building codes as a site of activism (Lui), the National Construction Code as a live lever for embodied-carbon reduction (Hill), and a reframing of risk itself (the Fire, Water, Building exhibition).
5. **Procurement** — the newest cluster, built from independent research because Weeks 1–4 barely mention procurement; drawn with an unfinished connector to be honest about how recently and thinly it's connected to the rest of the matrix.
6. **Power Structure** — drawn last, smallest, and set behind the rest of the layout: the diagram's actual argument, made visually rather than just stated.

## Cluster-by-cluster

### Hub + Registration Pathway

The hub question and the required organising spine are unchanged in substance from the synthesis map's canonical SPINE cluster (Weeks 1–3), restyled here as five gated stages. The contingency loop (fail the exam, incomplete hours, interstate/overseas mutual recognition) and the exclusion path (Tribunal, discipline register) are both real features of the actual system, not invented detail — see `05-diagrams/week-03/01-diagram.md`'s REG cluster for the full sourced detail.

### Education, Employment, Legislation, Professional Bodies

These four clusters carry, respectively: education (incl. CPD, tied to Molander's "formative epistemic measure" reading from Week 3); employment (including Week 4's observation that the profession ceded project management scope in the 1960s–70s); legislation (the Architects Act, Building Act, NCC, Planning and Environment Act, and the s.17–18 duty-to-public clauses); and professional bodies (AIA, ARBV, AACA, Parlour, ArchiTeam, plus Week 4's distinction between regulation and *recognition* machinery — awards and media are a genuinely separate system from the Board's statutory one).

### Ethics & Public Value

This cluster answers "who does the profession actually serve." It runs Loos's 1910 claim (no good or bad architects, only degrees of guilt) through Bailey, Shaw and Bruhn's shared argument that recognition should serve public value rather than individual reputation, and closes on Stead's "searchlight or lantern" question — whether the profession's own record actually reflects the public it claims to serve. This cluster is the diagram's ethical conscience, and it feeds directly back into the hub question.

### Research & Innovation

This is the only cluster built substantially from Week 5 pre-reading material (not yet lectured — tagged clearly). Ann Lui's Harvard Design Magazine piece argues building codes are a site of *co-authorship*: gender-inclusive bathroom code amendments (2018), post–Triangle Shirtwaist Fire egress requirements (1911), disability-access requirements following the 504 Sit-In and ADA, and the removal of explicitly segregationist plumbing codes (1958–73) all changed the built environment's baseline rules through activism, not expert committees alone. Dan Hill's Reduction Roadmap makes the same point operationally for Australia today: building the government's 200,000-homes-a-year target at current practice would consume 200 percent of the country's entire emissions budget, and the proposed fix is a *regulatory* one — cascading embodied-carbon limits written into the National Construction Code, cutting the current average from 461.8 to 6.63 kgCO2e/m²·a by 2028. Both readings support the same diagram-level point: legislation is not a fixed backstage machine — it's an active, amendable site, and Research & Innovation is where that gets made visible.

### Registration Pathway's entrance framing (Trust + Risk)

Added from a class tutorial whiteboard, not a lecture slide. The board named three components of Trust (Autonomy, Discretion, Accountability) and framed Risk as owed to both the Public and the Profession, growing with knowledge and experience. Framed as the pathway's entrance signage rather than a separate cluster, because that's structurally what it is: it doesn't add a new required topic, it names what the Registration Pathway's five stages are actually testing underneath the procedural detail (exam marks, logged hours). It sharpens the diagram's own critical-position language — "trusted with the title Architect, and trusted by whom, for what" (the hub question) now has a direct answer: trusted with autonomy, discretion and accountability, in proportion to the risk that trust transfers onto the public and the profession.

### The Project Delivery Process

Also from the tutorial whiteboard, nested inside Employment because it operates at a different scale than everything else on the map — the Registration Pathway is walked once, in a career; the Delivery Process runs once per project, on a loop. It walks Feasibility through Concept/Sketch Design (from a client-and-stakeholder brief), Developed Design, Construction Documentation, and Contract Administration, gated twice by real external checkpoints — Town Planning/Council approval and Building Surveyor sign-off — with a feedback path back to the client rather than a clean finish line. This gives Week 3's NSCA performance-criteria list (previously just four named categories) an actual operational shape, and gives Legislation's abstract "legislation" cluster two concrete, project-level enforcement points it didn't have before.

### Procurement

Built entirely from `02-assessment-tasks/procurement-research-note.md`, tagged as independent research since Weeks 1–4 contain exactly one sourced mention of procurement (Week 4's closing "collective test" line). The Australian Institute of Architects' own 2019 National Novation Survey (266 practices, 484 novated design-and-construct projects) found "an enormous opportunity to address building quality and prevent risk through the procurement process" — the Institute's own evidence that novation can strip a *registered* architect of direct client contact, site access, and a voice in value management, without touching their registration status at all. This is a structurally different vulnerability from anything the Registration Pathway tests for, which is exactly why it deserves its own cluster rather than a footnote on Employment.

### Power Structure

This cluster is the synthesis of everything else, and it is deliberately the smallest, most visually recessive element in the diagram — its subordinate position IS the argument. It reuses Week 4's "TheyRule" reading (a small, overlapping group sits across university leadership, the Institute and the registration board at once) and sets it against two facts established elsewhere on the map: the Registration Pathway is exhaustively gated (5 requirements, 3 pathways, 3-part exam), while Legislation (who gets to amend the code — Lui, Hill) and Procurement (who keeps design authority after novation) are comparatively open to whoever has the most leverage in the room.

## Critical point (draft position for you to refine)

The profession is very good at guarding the one gate everyone can see. It is much less good at guarding the two gates that actually decide what gets built: who gets to write and amend the codes that set the baseline for every building in Victoria, and who keeps creative and technical control of a project once it moves from design into delivery. Lui's building-code examples show that gate can be moved by activism, not just by architects; the AIA's own novation research shows the delivery gate can quietly close on a fully registered architect without any Tribunal involved at all. A Disciplinary Matrix that only draws the registration pathway misses where a lot of the profession's real vulnerability — and its real opportunity to earn more public trust — actually sits.

## If someone asks "so what?"

This diagram argues that "being a good architect" cannot be fully answered by the registration system alone (Weeks 1–3), or by the recognition system alone (Week 4) — it also depends on two under-examined mechanisms: who is allowed to shape the rules everyone builds under (legislation, contestable per Week 5's pre-readings), and who retains actual authorship once a project leaves the drawing board (procurement/novation, barely covered anywhere in the unit's own material). That three-part argument — regulation, recognition, and retained authorship — is the strongest, most defensible reading of "the Disciplinary Matrix of Architecture" this toolkit currently supports.
