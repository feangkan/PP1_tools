## Overarching position (draft — accept / edit / replace)

> Architecture's collective, publicly-accountable mechanisms — registration, the duty to the public, open codes, independent review — work and must be defended. What is failing is who gets to decide the value of the profession's knowledge, its innovation, and its labour: that decision is being captured privately or individually instead of staying with the profession collectively. This shows sharpest in procurement and in working conditions, where the person carrying responsibility for an outcome no longer holds authority over it.

**Why this wording.** The first two sentences are the whole argument, and they're the ones to say out loud in the WIP presentation. The third sentence is a pointer, not a rule — it tells the reader where the sharpest evidence sits (Points 8–9) without asking every point to fit that exact frame. Points 1–5 support "collective mechanisms work"; Points 6–10 support "the value decision is being captured away from the profession."

**A distinction worth stating explicitly when you write it up** (so it doesn't read as inconsistent): Point 1 defends *individual professional judgement* as something worth testing and protecting — that's a skill claim. Point 9 argues an *individual can't fix a structural condition alone* — that's a bargaining-power claim. These are different claims about "the individual" and don't contradict each other; say so in one sentence if the two points sit near each other in your final draft.

Grouping option: straight **5 + 5**, or themed — "Mechanisms that work (1–5)" / "Where the value decision has been taken away (6–10)".

### Point 8 — Procurement must not be allowed to strip design authority

### Point 9 — Working conditions are a structural problem and need a collective instrument
*"We must treat conditions as a professional question with a collective mechanism, not a personal failing."*

| Step | Draft content | Source |
|------|---------------|--------|
| Current condition | Exhaustion, unpaid overtime and low pay are treated as individual problems — individual resilience (a wellbeing seminar), individual negotiation, or individual escape (start a practice, go client-side, into development, project management or academia). Of the Wellbeing of Architects research's five areas — value and worth, time and money, trust and transparency, risk and responsibility, psychological/psychosocial safety — **only the last is about the individual; the other four describe how work is organised.** The race to the bottom is structural: two good practices competing for the same project cannot unilaterally stop cutting fees. The UAW exists but has no formal bargaining power; the award's overtime provisions have grey areas, and the "better off overall" test lets an above-award salary absorb unpaid overtime across a year. `[verify all figures — see week-07-bibliography.md]` | Week 7 |
| Position | **Change.** The individual is made responsible for a condition they have no authority to change — this is the same pattern as Point 8, applied to labour instead of design control. **Note the distinction from Point 1:** individual *judgement* is a skill worth protecting; individual responsibility for a *structural* condition is not the same claim, and it's the one being named here. | Week 7 |
| Mechanism | The modern award and the Fair Work Commission (clarify the overtime grey areas); enterprise/sector bargaining; fee benchmarking as a condition of public procurement; the Institute and ARBV treating conditions as a professional matter — they determine whether Point 1's supervision is actually possible. | Week 7 |
| Actor | UAW, ACA `[note: not the same interest as UAW — the ACA has contributed to wellbeing research and separately argued at the Fair Work Commission against overtime protections; verify before citing]`, the Fair Work Commission, government as procurer, ARBV, practices. | Week 7 |
| Action | Clarify the award's overtime provisions at the Commission; recognise a worker body in sector consultation; benchmark fees in public tenders. | proposal |
| Consequence | Matches the problem to a mechanism capable of changing it — "professional literacy" in the lecture's terms. Precedent: FAF turned private frustration into statistics, an open letter, then workplace organising and institutional governance (Muyiwa Oki's 2022 RIBA election). `[verify signatory count and election year]` **Tension worth writing:** a visible win is not a shift in power; about half of Australian architectural workers are in practices of five or fewer, where organising is hardest. | Week 7 |

### Point 10 — The profession must claim authority over where the value of its work goes
*"It is vital that we decide where the value of our work goes, rather than letting competition decide it."*

| Step | Draft content | Source |
|------|---------------|--------|
| Current condition | A typical architectural fee on a large project is around **2%** of construction cost; roughly **25%** of the billable rate charged against a worker's name returns to them (the four-times-salary multiplier); practices target around **20%** margin; developers require **20–30%** or won't build. Fee scales last applied in Australia in **1994**, ended as anti-competitive price-fixing — after which tendering rewards the hungriest firm. The benefit of any efficiency gain, AI included, currently defaults to whoever owns the tool or wins on price; nobody in the profession decides this, competition does. `[verify all figures — see week-07-bibliography.md]` | Week 7; Week 6 §D–E |
| Position | **Change.** The profession carries responsibility for the quality of the outcome while holding no authority over the price, the programme, or where the efficiency benefit lands. A price floor isn't available (fee scales can't legally return) — the credible move is visibility plus the conditions floor Point 9 argues for. **Where AI sits here, and only here:** it's one example of an efficiency gain among others; the argument is about the decision, not the tool. | Week 7; Week 6 §D |
| Mechanism | Fee/resourcing transparency in public design tenders; procurement rules that decline abnormally low bids on the same logic already used for construction tenders `[verify whether this provision exists for design services]`; a professional norm on how efficiency gains are accounted for. | proposal |
| Actor | Government clients (procurement conditions), AIA (norm + guidance), ACA/UAW (the labour side of the same question), practices. | — |
| Action | Government requires resourcing transparency on public design tenders and declines bids that cannot be lawfully delivered; the Institute publishes guidance on where production savings are expected to land. | — |
| Consequence | Underbidding stops being invisible; "who captures the benefit" becomes a decision rather than a residue of competition. **Tension:** commercial confidentiality; and transparency alone changes nothing without Point 9's enforced floor — the two only work together. | Week 6 §D; Week 7 |
