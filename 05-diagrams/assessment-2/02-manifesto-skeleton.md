## Overarching position (draft — accept / edit / replace)

> Architecture makes individuals responsible — for the public who are not in the room, for compliance with rules they had no route to write, for a design they may no longer control, for conditions they cannot change alone — and then allows authority over each of those things to sit somewhere else. Where responsibility and authority sit together, the profession's mechanisms work and must be defended. Where they have been separated, that is where reform belongs.

**Why this spine.** Assessment 1's critical position was that *the profession polices its one visible gate (registration) far more tightly than its less visible ones — who writes the code, who keeps design control after novation.* A1 asked **where power is mismatched**. A2 now **judges each mismatch and proposes the repair.** This is an extension of A1's own argument, not a new subject — which is what the brief's "credible within the professional system you mapped in Assessment 1" criterion asks for.

**Why it is not anti-individual.** The individual architect is the protagonist. The claim is *not* that individual judgement doesn't matter — it is the profession's core asset. The claim is that individual judgement can only be exercised where the individual has authority to match the responsibility they already carry. Molander's framing (Week 3) is the theory: discretion is deliberately delegated to a person the public can hold responsible, and accountability measures exist to make that discretion trustworthy — not to remove it.

**The spine test for every point:** state it as *"X carries the responsibility; Y holds the authority."* If a point can't be stated that way, it doesn't belong.

Grouping option: straight **5 + 5**, or themed — "Where the two are joined (1–5) · Where they have been separated (6–10)".

### Point 10 — The profession must claim authority over where the value of its work goes
*"It is vital that we decide where the value of our work goes, rather than letting competition decide it."*

| Step | Draft content | Source |
|------|---------------|--------|
| Current condition | On a large project a typical architectural fee is around **2%** of construction cost. Inside the practice, the four-times-salary multiplier in the AIA's practice notes means a person paid $50/hr is charged out near $200/hr — roughly **25%** of the billable rate against their name — with a target company margin around **20%**. A developer will not build below a **20–30%** margin. And the profession's own instrument for price is gone: **fee scales last applied in Australia in 1994**, abolished as anti-competitive price-fixing, after which tendering rewards the hungriest firm. Innovation is any shortening of production — and the benefit flows by default to whoever owns the tool, adopts first, or wins on price. Nobody in the profession decides where that value goes; competition decides it. `[verify the 2% / 25% / 20% / 20–30% figures and the 1994 fee-scale date against primary sources — these are lecture figures; cite the lecture unless verified]` | Week 7 L259–273, L285–295; Week 6 §D–E |
| Position | **Change.** The profession carries responsibility for the quality of the outcome while holding no authority over the price, the programme, or where the efficiency benefit lands. **Be careful here — this is where the point earns its marks:** the obvious proposal, bringing back fee scales, is not available. They were abolished as anti-competitive price-fixing and restoring them would require unwinding competition law, which is neither credible nor the argument. ## The AI stance (cross-cutting — not a point)

AI is kept in the manifesto as an **assistive instrument**, never as a subject. It supports production while the architect operates and decides. Stated once here so the position stays consistent wherever it surfaces:

1. **Responsibility cannot be delegated to something that cannot be held accountable.** Registration and the Act place responsibility on a person the public can hold to account; a tool cannot occupy that position. So the authority to decide stays with the person who carries the responsibility. *This is the manifesto's spine applied to tools* (Points 1, 2).
2. **Assisted work therefore passes through the same mechanisms as any other work** — supervision (Point 1), independent review (Point 3), and the judgement CPD is supposed to build (Point 5).
3. **The reverse-centaur test** (Gwyl Jahn, Week 6): the human drives and the tool is horsepower. If the tool decides and the human executes, responsibility and authority have separated again — the manifesto's exact failure mode, in miniature.
4. **Confidence is not judgement** (Week 6): a tool produces an answer without the disciplinary knowledge to judge whether it is appropriate — an argument *for* the second set of eyes, not against it.
5. **Efficiency gains are a profession-level decision** (Point 10), and **tool enclosure repeats standards enclosure** (Point 6, one line).

> Deliberately **not** argued: whether AI is good or bad for architecture. That framing invites a personal-opinion piece; the brief rewards mechanisms. Background material on both sides is retained in `../../02-assessment-tasks/ai-technology-research-note.md` if a tutor asks.
