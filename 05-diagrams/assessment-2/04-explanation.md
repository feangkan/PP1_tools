## 1. What the manifesto is

Ten propositions about how the profession of architecture should be practised, split five to maintain or refine and five to change, forming one position rather than ten observations. The brief's own whole-manifesto test is the thing to keep in view: *can a reader identify your overarching position, and do the ten points relate to one another?*

## 2. The position — responsibility without authority

The claim: **architecture is very good at placing responsibility on an individual, and much less good at making sure that same individual holds the authority to act on it.**

Responsibility arrives through several instruments at once. Registration makes a named person accountable. The *Architects Act 1991* ss.17–18 impose duties to the public. Professional indemnity insurance attaches financial consequence to judgement. None of that is in dispute.

Authority is the other half, and it is distributed far less carefully. The rules an architect is judged against are written in committees that aren't visible. The design an architect is legally responsible for can be taken out of their control by a contract. The price and programme that determine whether the work can be done properly are set by competition. The conditions under which the work happens are set by a market no individual practice controls.

So the position is not "architects should have more power." It is narrower and more defensible: **where responsibility and authority sit together, the mechanism works and should be defended; where they have been separated, that separation is the problem to fix.**

Two things this framing buys you. It keeps the individual architect as the protagonist — you are not arguing that individual judgement is unimportant, you are arguing it should be matched with the authority to exercise it. And it gives you a single test that sorts all ten points, which is what turns a list into a manifesto.

## 3. Where it comes from — Assessment 1

Assessment 1 concluded that the profession polices its one *visible* gate — registration — far more tightly than its *invisible* ones: who writes the code, and who keeps design control after novation.

That was a description of where power is mismatched. The manifesto turns each mismatch into a judgement and a proposed repair. This matters for marks as well as for coherence: the rubric asks whether your proposals are *credible within the professional system you mapped in Assessment 1*, so every point should be traceable to a cluster of that matrix rather than imported from outside it.

## 4. The test, and why the split is 5 and 5

One question, asked ten times: **does the person carrying the responsibility hold the authority to act on it?**

- Together → **maintain or refine.** The mechanism is doing its job; defend it, and name any specific refinement.
- Separated → **change.** Describe the current condition accurately first, then say what should change, who would act, and through what instrument.

The discipline this imposes: you cannot put a mechanism in the maintain column because you approve of it, and you cannot put one in the change column because it frustrates you. The sorting is done by the test.

## 5. The five to maintain or refine

**1 · Registration.** The clearest case where the two are deliberately joined: the registered architect is the one accountable *and* the one the law permits to act. The pathway — accredited education, ~3,300 supervised hours against 35 NSCA competencies, the APE with its national paper and interview, then ARBV registration with CPD and PI insurance — tests judgement rather than recall. The refinement: supervised experience only means anything if supervision is real, which depends on conditions (point 9) and on whether the supervisor still holds authority on the job (point 8).

**2 · Duty to the public.** Read ss.17–18 as *standing*, not merely obligation. The Act, backed by the Board, means that refusing to do something improper isn't simply an individual losing a client — it is a duty with institutional weight behind it. That is exactly Beaton's test, that professional codes demand conduct "beyond the personal moral obligations of an individual." Tom Marshall's Week 7 answer extends the same duty inward: as much as you care about the occupant you're designing for, what about the people producing the building?

**3 · Independent review.** Council planning and Building Surveyor sign-off deliberately place authority with someone *other* than the person responsible for the design. This is the one point where the spine's logic runs the other way and should — nobody checks their own work, and the separation is the safeguard. It also gives you the cleanest line on assisted documentation: a tool produces an answer with confidence but cannot be accountable for it, so the second set of eyes matters more, not less.

**4 · The split apparatus.** The AIA advances the discipline, the ARBV regulates, and they are separate by design. Parlour, ArchiTeam, Architects Declare, the ACA and the UAW fill what neither covers — above all by turning private experience into public evidence, which is what the Wellbeing of Architects research does. The refinement is to recognise and resource them. The honest tension to write, rather than hide: these bodies hold evidence but not authority, and they don't share one interest — the ACA co-produced the wellbeing research *and* has argued at the Fair Work Commission against overtime protections.

**5 · Codes and CPD.** The NCC and Australian Standards remain amendable through evidence and activism — Lui on egress rules and gender-inclusive bathrooms, Hill on the embodied-carbon roadmap. This is the one place practitioners still hold authority over the rules that bind them, which is why it belongs in the maintain column. The refinement is CPD: registration *assumes* judgement, so CPD should build it rather than tick boxes — including, on Marshall's evidence, how a fee is actually built, because you cannot exercise authority over a system you can't read.

## 6. The five to change

**6 · Enclosed knowledge.** Standards Australia was progressively sold to SAI Global; advisory notes became paywalled; flammable-cladding performance-test data is now private. You are held responsible for complying with documents you must pay to read. Because a national standards body once worked as a public good and was hollowed out, this is *restoration, not invention* — which is how you satisfy the brief's instruction not to propose a mechanism that already exists unless you explain why the existing one is inadequate.

**7 · Rule-making.** Entry to the profession is policed tightly; amendment of the codes is loosely gated — amendable, in effect, by whoever shows up — with a small group sitting across schools, the Institute and the Board simultaneously. You are bound by, and responsible for, rules you had no visible route to influence. The change is to the opacity and the concentration, not to the existence of the committees: publish membership and interests, disclose conflicts, limit cross-appointments. Name no individuals without evidence.

**8 · Procurement.** Novated design-and-construct has been standard in Victorian government and commercial work for over twenty years. The Institute's own 2019 National Novation Survey — 266 practices, 484 projects — found "an enormous opportunity to address building quality and prevent risk," and identified the mechanisms: lost contact with the original client, restricted site access, exclusion from value management. The architect stays professionally and legally responsible for the design while losing authority over it, **by contract alone, without losing registration.** The voluntary Code and Deed of Novation are inadequate precisely *because* they are voluntary, so the move is to make a strengthened Deed a standard condition of government procurement.

**9 · Conditions.** The profession treats exhaustion, unpaid overtime and low pay as individual problems — individual resilience, individual negotiation, or individual escape into development, project management or out of architecture altogether. But of the Wellbeing of Architects' five areas — value and worth, time and money, trust and transparency, risk and responsibility, psychosocial safety — only the last is about the individual; the other four describe how work is *organised*. And the race to the bottom is structural: two good practices bidding for the same project cannot unilaterally stop cutting fees, because neither controls what the other charges. There is a second-order cost worth naming: if everyone's answer to a structural problem is individual escape, the profession loses the experienced people who carry its collective knowledge — a disciplinary problem, not just a retention one. The instruments are the award and the Fair Work Commission, collective bargaining, fee benchmarking in public procurement, and the Board and Institute treating resourcing and supervision as professional-standard matters. The Australian precedent is the Builders Labourers Federation green bans: a union that organised first against deaths on site and then exercised authority over what got built.

**10 · Where the value goes.** A typical architectural fee on a large project is around 2% of construction cost; the four-times-salary multiplier means roughly a quarter of a billable rate returns to the person doing the work; developers won't build below a 20–30% margin. And the profession's own instrument for price is gone — fee scales last applied in Australia in 1994, abolished as anti-competitive. That constraint is what makes this point sophisticated rather than naïve: **don't propose restoring fee scales**, because that would require unwinding competition law. Propose transparency instead — a client who can't see how a fee was built can't tell a lean bid from an undeliverable one — resting on the enforced conditions floor from point 9. This is also where technology sits: efficiency gains from any tool should be a profession-level decision about where the benefit lands.

## 7. Point 9 is the new ground

Everything else in the manifesto extends Assessment 1 or the earlier lectures. Point 9 is the one that did not exist a fortnight ago, and it is the one the Week 1 lecture manifesto does not cover at all. It is therefore both your strongest claim to an original position and the point most likely to be challenged — lead with it, and be ready to defend it as a *professional* argument rather than an industrial one. The defence: conditions determine whether supervision is possible, and supervision is what registration is worth.

## 8. Why points 8 and 9 are one argument

This is the connection that turns ten points into a manifesto. Novation has resisted twenty years of advocacy through voluntary instruments. Marshall's account of why is blunt — locking the design at novation, or even being paid for the redocumentation a variation causes, is "a pipe dream in architecture because no one has any power to ask for that." So point 8 names the right repair and point 9 supplies the only instrument likely to deliver it. State this out loud; it is the single clearest piece of evidence that your points need each other.

The other dependencies worth stating: registration (1) assumes supervision, which assumes conditions (9) and authority on site (8); the duty to the public (2) is what the design-authority claim (8) rests on; an open amendment pathway (5) feeding a paywalled standard (6) is half a mechanism; paywalled standards (6) and invisible committees (7) are the same gate; and transparency over fees (10) changes nothing without an enforced floor (9).

## 9. Where AI sits

AI was cut as a manifesto point deliberately. It occupied half the first draft, and it weakened the argument: "is AI good or bad for architecture" invites an opinion piece, while the rubric rewards mechanisms, actors and pathways.

It survives as a stance, stated once: **a tool assists production but cannot be held responsible for the work, so the authority to decide stays with the person who can.** That is the manifesto's own spine applied to tools, which is why it doesn't need a slot of its own. It surfaces inside three points — independent review (3), enclosure repeating itself in proprietary tools (6), and where efficiency gains land (10). The reverse-centaur idea from Week 6 is the failure mode in miniature: if the tool decides and the human executes, responsibility and authority have separated again.
