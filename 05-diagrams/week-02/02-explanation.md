## Cluster-by-cluster

### Traits model — evidence pointers

- **What it means:** The lecture repeats Week 1's five traits (disciplinary knowledge, regulation, autonomy, altruism, higher learning) but adds a method: place the registered architect at the centre, put the five traits around it, then go find *evidence* for each — ARBV/Acumen for regulation, the AQF master's descriptors for higher learning, and so on.
- **Why on diagram:** It is the lecturer's own suggested Assessment 1 diagramming method, stated directly in the recording.
- **Links:** Feeds the registration spine; overlaps heavily with Week 1's core loop, which is why v02 may merge it with Continuum.

### Continuum model — Goode and Moore

- **What it means:** Instead of a checklist, this model places occupations on a spectrum from skilled trade (repeatable competence, e.g. a potter) to profession (judgment on a novel, prioritised problem). Architecture is tested against building practitioners, project managers, and the nurse/doctor comparison, where advancing technology has blurred who exercises judgment.
- **Why on diagram:** It directly answers "what's actually unique to architects?" — a sharper question than the traits list alone.
- **Links:** Contrasts with Traits (qualities vs judgment-intensity); connects to Apparatus via the question of who is trusted to exercise judgment.

### Apparatus — institutions, laws, power

- **What it means:** The profession is a network of interested bodies — ARBV, AACA, AIA, universities, unions, government — each with different hierarchies and interests, governing architecture through Acts, Regulations, Codes, and Contracts. The lecture poll asked who holds the greatest influence over a final building (architect, client, builder, regulator, or finance) — most students split across all five, and the lecturer suggested influence may sit *in the relationship between actors*, not in any one of them.
- **Why on diagram:** This is the "who holds power, and is that where it should sit?" question carried over from Week 1's class discussion.
- **Links:** Feeds the registration spine (ARBV enables registration); constrains Kuhn's paradigm shift (institutional inertia slows change).

### Kuhn — Structure of Scientific Revolutions

- **What it means:** Normal practice accumulates small anomalies (a cracked slab here, a cladding issue there); once anomalies become systemic they form a crisis; only a crisis can force a genuine paradigm shift, after which a new "normal" is established. The lecture polled the class on where architecture currently sits — most answered "crisis," few "revolution" — and noted that those currently holding institutional power often have the least incentive to drive the shift.
- **Why on diagram:** It explains *why* the profession needs to evolve, not just *that* it should.
- **Links:** Dashed line to Divided Line — crisis conditions (Lacrosse Tower, Opal/Mascot-style defect cases) show what happens when the instrument chain (contract → construction) fails.

### Plato's Divided Line — abstract ↔ physical

- **What it means:** Everything above the line is abstract (laws, disciplinary knowledge, IP, the "idea" of a building); everything below is physical (the constructed building, materials, labour). Drawings and the contract sit **on** the line — they are the instruments that translate an abstract idea into something a bank can value, a builder can construct, and a court can hold someone accountable to. Once built, a building only re-enters disciplinary knowledge if it is written about, awarded, or studied as a precedent — otherwise the innovation is lost.
- **Why on diagram:** It is the lecture's clearest answer to "how is an architect more than someone who does drawings?" — the architect manages the whole abstract-to-physical translation, not just the drawing itself.
- **Links:** Feeds into the registration spine (the contract/drawing pair is the actual instrument of practice); connects to copyright/IP and market mechanisms (not detailed on this diagram — see topics-context for scope note).

### Sciulli — structural and institutional invariance (with case study)

- **What it means:** Sciulli argues professions are distinguished from other occupations by **eight structural qualities** (operating within structured situations of power/dependence, an independent socio-cultural authority, fiducial responsibilities to both clients and the wider social order, dual occupational orientations, collegial governance, merit-based entry, and patrolled jurisdictions) and that professionalism always introduces **four institutional consequences** into the wider society (a governance structure, upgraded public discourse, restrained positional power, and — specifically — support for democratic institutional design over despotic or merely formal-democratic alternatives).
- **The case, as requested:** Sciulli's central historical case is the **Paris visual Academie** (Académie Royale de Peinture et de Sculpture, founded 1648). By 1666–80 it had built the first Western "professional graduate school" (its Rome satellite), admission and advancement decided by anonymous, judged student competitions rather than aristocratic patronage, and a formal conference series that standardised a shared visual "lexicon." It professionalised **two full centuries before English law or any architecture profession**. Sciulli notes that, before this, aristocrats "drew or designed their own residential architecture" — architecture itself sat *outside* structured professional situations, making the Academie's break from patronage-based credentialing a useful mirror for how architecture's own registration system claims to work today.
- **Why on diagram:** This is the assigned reading's own organising framework, worked through with its actual case rather than only the abstract's two claims.
- **Links:** Green "structural evidence" link to Assessment 1 — Sciulli's meritocratic entry/jurisdiction argument maps directly onto the Victoria registration spine (accredited education → logbook → exam → registration = a modern structured, merit-gated pathway).

## Critical point (draft position for you to refine)

Week 2's frameworks converge on one claim: architecture's legitimacy as a profession rests on **structured, merit-gated, judgment-bearing positions** (Sciulli, Continuum) operating inside a **contested network of institutional power** (Apparatus) that is currently accumulating anomalies toward crisis (Kuhn), and that only becomes real through **instruments** — drawings and contracts — that translate abstract knowledge into accountable physical outcomes (Divided Line). If Assessment 1's registration spine is read only as a procedural checklist, it misses this: the spine is legitimate specifically because it replicates Sciulli's meritocratic, structured-situation logic, not because a regulator simply says so.
