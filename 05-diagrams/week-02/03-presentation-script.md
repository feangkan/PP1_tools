# 03 — Presentation script: Week 02 — Models for Understanding a Profession

- **Paired diagram:** `01-diagram.md`
- **Time target:** 3–4 minutes
- **Tone:** spoken, clear

---

## Hook (20 sec)

"This diagram is my Week 2 map of **six different ways to understand architecture as a system**, not just a list of traits. It responds directly to my tutor's Week 3 email, which named Week 2's real topic as 'conceptual frameworks for organising complex systems' — so that's what I've built."

---

## Walk the diagram (2–2.5 min)

"**Start at the centre.** Every framework this week answers one question: what organising principle explains the profession as a system?

"**Top-left, the Traits model.** Same five traits as Week 1 — disciplinary knowledge, regulation, autonomy, altruism, higher learning — but this week's lecture gave a method: put the architect in the centre, the traits around it, then go find evidence. Where's the regulation? ARBV, the Act. Where's higher learning? The AQF's master's-level descriptors.

"**Continuum model, next to it.** This one's sharper — it's not about qualities, it's about judgment. A potter can repeat a technique perfectly; that's skill. A professional makes a determination on a novel problem. That's the line between a draftsperson and an architect, or between a nurse and a doctor — and that line is shifting as technology changes who actually exercises judgment.

"**Left side, Apparatus.** The profession is a network — ARBV, AACA, AIA, university, government — each with different interests and hierarchies. The lecture polled us on who holds the most influence over a final building. We split across client, builder, regulator, architect, finance — and the answer offered was: maybe influence isn't in any one of them, it's in the relationship between them.

"**Red box, Kuhn.** Normal practice accumulates small anomalies — a cracked slab, a cladding failure — until they become systemic, a crisis, and only then does real change happen. The class was polled on where architecture sits right now. Most said crisis. Almost nobody said revolution.

"**Right side, the Divided Line.** Everything abstract — laws, codes, disciplinary knowledge — sits above the line. Everything physical — the building, the labour — sits below. Drawings and the contract sit *on* the line: they're the instruments that turn an idea into something a bank will fund and a builder will construct. And once it's built, it only re-enters knowledge if someone writes about it, publishes it, or it wins an award.

"**Green cluster, Sciulli.** The assigned reading. Eight structural qualities, four institutional consequences — but the case that makes it real is the Paris visual Academie, founded 1648. It ran anonymous, judged student competitions instead of aristocratic patronage, and professionalised two centuries before English law did. Before that, Sciulli notes, aristocrats simply designed their own residential architecture — there was no structured profession at all. That's a direct mirror for what architecture's own registration pathway claims to do today.

"**Blue cluster, bottom — my tutor's design brief.** Frameworks to think with, not copy. Registration pathway as the spine. Legible at three distances. I've tried to apply all three here, and I'll rebuild v02 around one dominant spine rather than six equal branches, the way OMA's diagrams work."

---

## Critical takeaway (40 sec)

"The throughline for me is: the registration spine in Assessment 1 isn't just a compliance checklist. Sciulli's argument is that structured, merit-gated positions with real judgment are what make a profession legitimate in the first place — that's the same logic behind education, logbook, exam, and registration. And Kuhn's model tells me that spine has to be able to change, not stay fixed, or the profession stalls in denial while anomalies pile up into crisis."

## Backup answers

| Likely question | Short answer |
|-----------------|---------------|
| Why six frameworks, not one? | The tutor's email frames Week 2 as "organising complex systems" — the point is comparing lenses, not picking a single answer yet. |
| Is the Sciulli case actually about architecture? | Not directly — it's about painting/sculpture in 17th-century Paris — but Sciulli explicitly notes architecture was, at the time, undertaken by aristocrats outside any structured profession, which is the useful contrast. |
| Why does Kuhn matter for Assessment 1? | It gives a mechanism (not just an opinion) for why the profession needs to evolve — useful for the "how might it evolve" part of the brief. |
| What's different about v02? | One dominant spine (registration pathway) instead of six equal radial branches, following the tutor's "spine, not a flat list" precedent (OMA/Koolhaas). |
