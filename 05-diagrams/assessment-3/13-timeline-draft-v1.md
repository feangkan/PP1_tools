## Phase 1 — Client brief to tender (1956–1958)

### 1. The competition begins — 1956
The New South Wales Government opens an international design competition for a new opera house at Bennelong Point. This is the very start of the project — before there's a client relationship with any one architect, before there's a site strategy, before there's a brief in the normal sense. It's the event everything else in the file grows out of.
*Detail note: confirmed fact, matches the syllabus. No form number — this predates any of the contract documents.*

### 2. The architect sends an introductory letter — early 1957 (exact date not yet fixed)
Once the competition is won, the architect writes to the client introducing themselves formally and proposing the terms of engagement — this is the letter that leads into the Client Architect Agreement. It's a short, standard piece of professional correspondence, but it's the first document in the file where the architect speaks to the client directly and in writing.
*Detail note: this event is already a row in the Register but has no date yet. Suggest placing it a few weeks before the CAA is signed, i.e. early February 1957. Not yet fixed — needs a group decision.*

### 3. The Client Architect Agreement is signed — 21 February 1957
This is the contract that formally appoints the architect and sets out the scope of services, the fee, and how the working relationship runs. It's one of the two central contracts in the whole file (the other is the construction contract, later).
*Detail note: this is a real, dated event in the Register. But there's a mismatch worth fixing — the Register currently labels this "CAA 2019," while the assessment specifically requires the 2024 edition of the agreement. This isn't a typo to quietly correct; it means whoever is drafting the actual CAA document needs to be working from the 2024 template, not the 2019 one. Flag it, don't just relabel it.*

## Phase 2 — Tender to start of construction (1958–1959)

### 4. Tender documents go out — 1958
The architect issues the tender documents to prospective contractors, so they can price the works and bid for the job. This is the architect acting on the client's behalf to set the construction procurement process in motion.
*Detail note: confirmed in the Register. No form number attached — this is a general tender document, not one of the numbered ABIC forms.*

### 5. Tenders come back — 1959
Contractors return their priced tenders to the architect for assessment. This is a short but important beat — it's the moment the actual cost of construction starts to become real, rather than an early estimate.
*Detail note: confirmed in the Register.*

### 6. The contractor is selected — 1959
Following the tender review, one contractor is chosen. From here on, that contractor is a party to the file.
*Detail note: confirmed in the Register.*

### 7. The client changes representative — 1959 (exact date not yet fixed)
Somewhere around this point, the person representing the client changes: Premier Cahill hands over to Premier Askin. This matters more than it might first sound, because the Client Architect Agreement explicitly allows either party to replace its representative, provided it gives notice — so this is a real, contractually meaningful event, not just background history.
*Detail note: this event isn't in the Register yet. It comes from the tutor's own video, which simplifies the whole of the client's side to just two people — Cahill for the first two years, then Askin for the rest. The exact date of the handover still needs to be pinned down or agreed as a placeholder. This is a Lead 1 item to add.*

### 8. The construction contract is signed — 19 February 1959
The client and the contractor sign the Simple Works Contract 2018 (the "ABIC SW"), with the architect administering it. From this point on there are two live contracts running in parallel through the file: the Client Architect Agreement (client–architect) and the Simple Works Contract (client–contractor).
*Detail note: confirmed in the Register.*

### 9. Construction begins — Stage I commences, 1959
Site works start. This is where the Contract thread shifts from paperwork to a physical building going up.
*Detail note: confirmed in the Register. The exact site-possession date isn't yet checked against the official Sydney Opera House project information — earlier work in this repo assumed 2 March 1959, but that still wants verifying.*

## Phase 3 — Construction to completion (1961–1974)

### 10. The design problem emerges — 1961
The roof's structural geometry is finalised, and it turns out to demand more of the podium columns than they were originally designed to carry. This is the moment the "something changed" question actually gets triggered — everything from here to the final certificate is the architect and contractor working out what to do about it.
*Detail note: not yet a Register row on its own — it's really the reason behind events 11 and 12. Still needs checking against the project information.*

### 11. The architect tells the client the design has grown — 1961 (exact date not yet fixed)
Before (or alongside) instructing the contractor, the architect is obliged to tell the client that this redesign counts as extra architectural services, and that it will affect the fee. This is the quiet, easy-to-miss half of the story: everyone remembers to update the construction contract when something changes, but the agreement with the client also has to be updated, in writing, and that's the architect's job, not the contractor's.
*Detail note: not yet in the Register. This is the Client Architect Agreement's mirror of the construction variation below — one is about the building, this one is about the architect's own work and fee. Belongs to Lead 1.*

### 12. The architect instructs a variation — 1961 (SW-12)
The architect formally instructs the contractor to strengthen the columns and add extra piers, to cope with the higher loads from the finished roof design. This is the first of the four required contract-administration forms, and it's the moment the "extra piers" scenario becomes a real, documented instruction rather than just a design realisation.
*Detail note: confirmed in the Register (already logged by Prani, checked by Jerry). Under the tutor's own worked example for this exact scenario, the contractor's response to this instruction is a request for one extra month and $5,000 (labour and concrete) — those are the numbers this file should use unless the group decides otherwise.*

### 13. The time extension is granted — 1961 (SW-14)
Following the contractor's claim, the architect formally adjusts the completion date by one month to cover the extra structural work.
*Detail note: confirmed in the Register.*

### 14. The cost plan is issued — 1962
The quantity surveyor sends the architect an updated cost plan, reflecting where the project's budget stands. This sits alongside the price adjustment below rather than being part of the same instruction.
*Detail note: confirmed in the Register. An earlier course sample put the figure at roughly A$27.5 million — worth confirming that still applies here.*

### 15. The contract price is adjusted — 1962 (SW-11)
The architect formally increases the contract price by $5,000 to cover the cost of the additional piers and strengthened columns — the direct financial consequence of the variation instructed in event 12.
*Detail note: confirmed in the Register.*

### 16. Stage II begins — 1962
With the podium issue resolved, construction moves into its second stage, and the architect issues the Stage II contract drawings to the contractor.
*Detail note: confirmed in the Register.*

### 17. Stage III begins — 1967
The project moves into its third and final construction stage; the Stage III contract drawings follow the year after, in 1968.
*Detail note: confirmed in the Register.*

### 18. Practical completion is certified — 1973
The architect certifies that the building has reached practical completion — substantially finished and ready for handover, even if minor items remain.
*Detail note: confirmed in the Register.*

### 19. The final certificate is issued — 1974 (SW-31)
The architect issues the final certificate, closing out the contract. This certificate has to expressly refer back to the price adjustment from event 15 — the file needs to show that final number tracing all the way back to the original instruction, with nothing lost or contradicted along the way.
*Detail note: confirmed in the Register.*
