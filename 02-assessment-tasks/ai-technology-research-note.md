# AI, emerging technology & innovation — research note (for Assessment 2)

**Status: mix of Week 6 lecture content (sourced) + independent research (to verify).** Per `pp1-toolkit.mdc` Hard Rule 1, lecture-sourced points are tagged `[W6]` and trace to `03-weekly-resources/week-06/`; everything else is independent background the student is choosing to bring in and must cite directly, not present as unit content. **Do not quote a statistic from this note without checking the primary source** — the Week 6 transcripts paraphrase several reports without citation.

Purpose: give Assessment 2 a defensible **position on technology and innovation** — one that weights the productive case for engaging with AI while being honest about the harms and, crucially, naming the **professional mechanisms** that would make the productive case real rather than assumed.

---

## 1. The overarching position (draft)

**The profession should engage with AI deliberately and early, and build the collective mechanisms that decide who captures its benefit — because refusing to engage cedes that decision to platforms and to the largest practices.**

Why this is the position, not "AI good" or "AI bad":

- `[W6]` Innovation is "any shortening of the production process"; AI is a large, fast instance of that. Shortening production is not itself professional progress — **the question is who captures the benefit** (client / practice / employee / tech company) and whether the profession learns from it. (`good-bad-architect-source.md` §D, §J)
- `[W6]` Competition removes the individual's choice: "the market is going to make that decision for you because you have to compete." So *where the value flows* is a **profession-level** question — a manifesto point — not a personal preference. (`entrepreneur-collective-source.md` Pt1 §framing)
- `[W6]` "the most important thing with AI is to just not ignore it. The risk of ignoring it is really high … the cost of engaging … is really low." Ignoring it also removes the profession's voice from the discourse. (`entrepreneur-collective-source.md` Pt2 §ethics)
- The Guardian's "AI absolutism" piece (set reading) argues the apocalyptic framing is itself a sales tactic — **the future is a set of choices, not an inevitability.** Weighting the pros ≠ naïveté; it's insisting the profession has agency here. (`03-weekly-resources/week-06/links.md`)
- The LSE "digital good" piece (set reading) frames the *good* as something that must be actively defined and pursued, not assumed as the default. That is exactly a manifesto's job.

## 2. The case FOR engaging (the pros — weight the manifesto here)

| Pro | Detail | Source |
|-----|--------|--------|
| **Leverage on real problems** | Use AI to solve a genuine, widely-held problem (make a tool, automate a task you'd rather not do, explore ten options instead of one) — *"know what needs doing" + "AI can do it" = benefit*. | `[W6]` `entrepreneur-collective-source.md` Pt1 §value, §what-to-do |
| **Automating your own computer work** | The transformative use isn't the chatbot — it's an agent operating your computer (formatting, searching, modelling, repetitive documentation). "nobody's doing it" — early adopters gain agency. | `[W6]` Pt1 §value |
| **Doing more architecture, not just cheaper architecture** | If a 10-hour task takes 1 hour, the *good* outcome is capacity to do work you previously couldn't — poll: ~20% were most excited about "greater capability, doing things I couldn't previously do". | `[W6]` `good-bad-architect-source.md` §J; Pt2 §polls |
| **Custom tools reduce dependence on rented black boxes** | "making software is now nearly free" — a practice can build its own modelling/workflow tools instead of renting proprietary ones, *if* it has the capacity. Potentially reverses part of the privatisation problem. | `[W6]` `entrepreneur-collective-source.md` Pt2 §privatised-tools |
| **Codes and standards as a lever** | AI lowers the cost of the research that feeds NCC/AS amendments (modelling, evidence synthesis) — the "collective machinery" could run faster if the outputs stay open. | Inference from `[W6]` §E + Assessment 1 Research & Innovation cluster (Lui, Hill) — mark for check |
| **Value rises for the human skills architecture teaches** | wisdom, judgement, initiative, agency, defending ideas, questioning whether you're solving the right problem — "skills that are really hard to get from other forms of education". Design resists automation because it redefines the problem while solving it. | `[W6]` Pt1 §value-up-down; Pt2 §education, §design-resists |

## 3. The case AGAINST / the harms (name these honestly — don't skip)

| Con | Detail | Source |
|-----|--------|--------|
| **Value capture defaults to the platform / large practice** | Efficiency gains flow to whoever owns the tool and can adopt it first; disparity between large and small practices widens. | `[W6]` Pt1 §framing; Pt2 §privatised-tools |
| **Confidence ≠ professional judgment** | AI produces an answer without the disciplinary knowledge to judge whether it's appropriate, correct, or in the public interest. | `[W6]` `good-bad-architect-source.md` §J |
| **The experience ladder is pulled up** | Repetitive graduate tasks automate first; the work through which juniors *acquire* judgement disappears; unpaid internships become the route to disciplinary knowledge. | `[W6]` Pt1 §what-to-do; Pt2 §graduate-jobs |
| **Reverse centaur** | If used uncritically, AI does the thinking and the human just executes — deskilling, not augmentation. | `[W6]` Pt1 §what-not-to-do |
| **Accountability dumped on the individual** | Current policy makes the individual (often a graduate told to go faster) responsible for checking AI output — Helen: "makes absolutely no sense". | `[W6]` Pt2 §who-checks |
| **Capitalist extraction** | Training data is scraped IP; value concentrates in a few firms (Anthropic, OpenAI). "the ultimate form of capitalist extraction." | `[W6]` Pt2 §ethics |
| **Environmental & infrastructural cost** | Data-centre water/energy use; siting near communities. Helen frames this as a *citizen* concern, real but distinct from the professional question. | `[W6]` Pt2 §ethics |
| **Bubble / instability risk** | LLM providers are heavily loss-making; some may fail. Capability persists even if firms don't (telecom-fibre analogy) — but tool availability and pricing are unstable. | `[W6]` Pt2 §bubble |
| **Black-box specification tools** | AI embedded in proprietary product-spec / BIM tools repeats the privatisation problem — knowledge accessible only if you pay. | `[W6]` Pt2 §privatised-tools |
| **Homogenisation of architectural discourse** | Generated ArchDaily-style articles, renders, models "most people wouldn't be able to tell the difference" — thin discourse gets thinner. | `[W6]` Pt1 §history |

## 4. The professional mechanisms that would make the pros real (this is the manifesto's substance)

The brief rewards proposals that name an **actor**, an **instrument** and a **pathway**. Candidates, drawn from Week 6 + Assessment 1:

| Proposed mechanism | Actor(s) | Instrument / pathway | Consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Collective checking of AI-assisted work** — a defined review step by an experienced architect, like an engineering documentation review | Practices; ARBV; AIA | Practice QA procedures; a Code of Conduct clause or ARBV guidance note making the *supervising* architect, not the graduate, accountable for AI-assisted output | Protects registration's meaning; preserves the mentoring relationship through which judgement is learned |
| **Shared / institute-held access to architecture-specific AI tools** | AIA; AACA; possibly ARBV | Membership benefit; a shared tool library or negotiated licensing; open-source reference implementations | Narrows the large-vs-small practice gap; keeps disciplinary knowledge inspectable |
| **A protected route to disciplinary experience** that doesn't rely on unpaid internships | Universities; ARBV; practices; government | Funded graduate positions tied to logbook competencies; APE pathway requirements that assume *supervised* AI use | Keeps the experience ladder intact as tasks automate |
| **Keep the "collective machinery" outputs open** — research feeding NCC/AS amendments stays public even when AI-produced | Standards bodies; universities; government | Procurement/funding conditions requiring open publication; resisting further Standards Australia-style privatisation | The profession keeps learning; the black box doesn't spread to the codes |
| **Disclosure of AI use and value capture at practice level** | AIA; ARBV; clients (esp. government) | Tender/engagement disclosure; CPD on AI literacy; the ARBV/RMIT study's findings feeding policy | Makes "who captures the benefit" visible and contestable |
| **CPD reframed as formative AI/judgement literacy**, not compliance ticking | ARBV; CPD providers | Mandatory CPD content on when *not* to use AI, how to check it, how to keep design authorship | Builds the judgement that distinguishes confidence from competence |

## 5. Ethics & profession — the through-line

- `[W6]` **Mechanisms over individual virtue.** "The Good and the Bad Architect" argues good practice should be supported by professional *mechanisms*, not left to individual ethics. Every AI point above should name a mechanism, not just an intention.
- `[W6]` **The client not in the room** — occupants, future users, the environment, the public — is the test for any AI proposal: does it serve them, or just make one architect more efficient / marketable? (This is Week 4's "collective test" applied to technology.)
- `[W6]` **Citizen hat vs architect hat** (Helen): extraction, data centres and environmental cost are real and belong in the argument, but the manifesto's *distinct* contribution is the professional question — accountability, value capture, and the meaning of registration when the work is AI-assisted.
- **Efficient / effective / ethical** (brief prompt): AI maximises *efficient*; the manifesto's job is to show where that trades against *effective* (does it produce good architecture / good outcomes) and *ethical* (who benefits, who checks, who is excluded).
- Beaton's test (brief): professionalism = collective structures + specialised knowledge + public responsibility + accountability mechanisms, "beyond the personal moral obligations of an individual." An AI position built only on "I will use it responsibly" fails this test; one built on review steps, shared access and disclosure passes it.
