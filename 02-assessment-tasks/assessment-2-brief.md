## Task in one sentence

Write a coherent **10-point manifesto** — a model for how you believe the profession of architecture should practise — each point a direct proposition ("We must…", "We will…", "It is vital that…") plus an accompanying paragraph, built by developing tensions and mechanisms already identified in Assessment 1.

## Required structure

- **10 points total**, splitting into two halves:
  - **5 points — mechanisms to MAINTAIN or REFINE.** Identify five mechanisms that currently work well and should remain (or be refined). Refer to where the mechanism actually exists, explain how it contributes positively, and judge whether to keep it as-is or refine it.
  - **5 points — mechanisms to CHANGE.** Identify five areas where the profession should change more substantially. Describe the current condition accurately first; don't propose a mechanism that already exists unless you explain why the existing one is inadequate.
- The 10 points must form **one coherent position on the profession**, not 10 unrelated observations. Grouping into themed sections with a shared paragraph is allowed; sub-points are allowed. First sentence ~10 words; then a developed paragraph.
- **Per-point thinking sequence** (a method, not required headings): **CURRENT CONDITION → POSITION → MECHANISM → ACTOR → ACTION → CONSEQUENCE**
  1. Current condition — what actually exists now? Verify before proposing change.
  2. Position — maintain, refine or change, and why.
  3. Mechanism — what professional instrument or pathway could support this position?
  4. Actor — who has the authority, responsibility or capacity to act?
  5. Action — what specifically would they do?
  6. Consequence — what changes in the profession or for the public if this occurs? Note tensions / unintended effects.

## What the assessment develops (from the brief)

- Understanding of how the profession currently operates — institutions, instruments, responsibilities, existing mechanisms.
- Connecting issues to responsible actors and professional mechanisms; explaining relationships, tensions and leverage points; showing how change could actually occur.
- Informed judgement about what to maintain, refine or change, and how proposals strengthen professional standards, collective responsibility and/or public value.
- A coherent manifesto with a clear overarching position, supported by lectures/readings and independent research, communicated directly and persuasively.

## Test-the-whole-manifesto checklist (from the brief)

- Can the reader identify your overarching position on how architecture should be practised?
- Do the 10 points relate to one another, or are they 10 interesting issues?
- Have you distinguished what should be retained from what requires reform?
- Are your proposals credible within the professional system you mapped in Assessment 1?
- Does your research support your argument, and have you explained the professional/public consequence?

## Extra prompts (lenses, not a checklist)

- "The Good and the Bad Architect" — what professional **mechanisms** support good practice, rather than relying only on individual ethics?
- Does a lecture present a process, an outcome, a way of thinking, or a new way of *describing* architecture? What could that contribute?
- How should other architects be treated, and how do you want to be treated as an architect? (workers' rights)
- **Efficient / effective / ethical** — what conflicts arise when these are balanced and prioritised against one another?
- Use professional traits, exemplars, paradigm shifts, public value, collective responsibility, professional judgement as *lenses where they genuinely help*, not as a checklist.

## Beaton's definition (the brief's test for your manifesto)

> "A profession is a disciplined group of individuals who adhere to ethical standards and hold themselves out as, and are accepted by the public as, possessing special knowledge… Such codes require behaviour and practice beyond the personal moral obligations of an individual." — George Beaton, *Why Professionalism Is Still Relevant* (2010).

Use critically — reinforce, challenge or extend it, but show that professionalism involves **collective structures, specialised knowledge, public responsibility and mechanisms of accountability**, not simply personal morality.
