## Why procurement belongs on the Disciplinary Matrix

Registration determines *who* may call themselves an architect. Procurement determines *how much control that architect actually retains* once a project moves from design into delivery. It is a genuine gap in Weeks 1–4's material and a real mechanism through which the profession's authority is either preserved or eroded — which makes it a strong candidate for the diagram's critical position, not just a box to tick.

## What procurement means (AIA definition)

The Australian Institute of Architects' Acumen practice notes define procurement as "the act or process of bringing about or bringing into existence buildings… the total process of bringing into being a building that was not there before." It can also refer specifically to "the acquiring and engagement of architectural services."

## Core Australian procurement methods

| Method | Standard form | Who controls design | Architect's role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Traditional (design–bid–build) | AS4000 | Client/architect design first, then tendered | Full design authorship, administers contract on client's behalf |
| Design and construct (D&C) | AS4902 | Contractor takes on design obligations, from a client-supplied Principal's Project Requirements brief | Reduced — architect may design only to a preliminary stage, or work for the contractor post-novation |
| Novated D&C | AS4902 (novation clause) | Client engages architect, then "novates" (transfers) that architect's engagement to the contractor mid-project | Architect's contractual client relationship switches to the contractor — the specific point Assessment 1 should scrutinise |
| Construction management / managing contractor, project alliancing, PPP | Various | Shared/negotiated | Varies; architect typically has less direct authority than traditional procurement |

Roughly 68 percent of Australian construction projects use a standard-form contract, most commonly AS4300 or AS4902 (design and construct) and AS4000 or AS2124 (construct-only).

## The specific critical tension: novation

Novated D&C has been standard practice in both government and commercial procurement in Victoria for more than twenty years. The AIA's own research is the strongest available critical-position material:

- The Institute's 2019 National Novation Survey covered 266 architect practices and 484 novated D&C projects nationally and found "an enormous opportunity to address building quality and prevent risk through the procurement process" — i.e., the Institute's own evidence base finds the current system is *not* adequately protecting design intent.
- The Institute states directly that "novation can diminish the original design intent, especially in value management during the construction (delivery) phase of a project," and identifies specific mechanisms: architects losing direct communication with the original client (principal), restricted site access, exclusion from project control meetings, and inability to participate in value-management decisions once novated to the contractor.
- In response, the Institute produced two policy tools: a **Code of Novation** (industry-wide conduct standards — documentation completeness at the novation point, contractor-selection protocols, superintendent independence during construction) and a **Deed of Novation** (an alternate legal instrument addressing the specific risks the survey identified).

## How this connects to the rest of your matrix

- **Registration/regulation (Weeks 1–3):** registration establishes *legal authority* to practise; procurement/novation is where that authority is *operationally* diluted, even for a fully registered architect — a genuinely different kind of vulnerability than the ones Weeks 1–3 cover.
- **Week 4's "collective test":** does a given procurement arrangement make architecture more useful, equitable, and publicly accountable, or does it just shift risk and control away from the architect who is professionally and legally responsible for the design? Novation is close to a textbook case for this test.
- **Public value / who is served:** under novation, the architect's contractual duty shifts from the original client to the contractor — directly relevant to Weeks 1 and 3's "who is the client not in the room" question, but from a commercial-structure angle rather than an ethics-clause angle.
